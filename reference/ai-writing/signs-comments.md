# Signs of AI use in comments: notes

Source: Wikipedia, "Wikipedia:Signs of AI use in comments" (listed as "Signs of AI-generated comments" in directories; WikiProject AI Cleanup). Retrieved 2026-11-01; revision 1382846732 (2026-10-30). Text © Wikipedia contributors, CC BY-SA 4.0. These notes are an adaptation (summarized, restructured) and are licensed CC BY-SA 4.0. Not an official Wikipedia policy.

Scope: signs in talk-page and discussion comments, deliberately excluding tells already covered on the main page. Much of it is specific to Wikipedia conflict handling; web-copy relevance is flagged. The transferable parts: assurance language, "I am happy to", "I respectfully", subject lines and letter formality, titled sections in short messages, chat-log leftovers, procedural over-explaining.

Structure: `###` = section of the source, `####` = subsection, `#####` = sub-subsection.

## Wikipedia:Signs of AI use in comments
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_use_in_comments

### Lead
- Comments suspected of being pasted from an LLM may be collapsed or closed. Using AI to write comments is considered disruptive and persistent users may be blocked.
- Many main-page tells (boldface, em dashes, curly apostrophes and quotation marks, negative parallelisms, vertical lists, Markdown, rule of three) often appear in comments too. This page lists only tells that usually do not appear in article or draft text. Overly procedural language and "spelling out" of core policies can also appear in edit summaries.
- The list is descriptive, not prescriptive.

### Content
#### Overassurance of quality or goodwill
Words to watch: `aligns with Wikipedia's goals/mission`, `I am committed to ...`, `I assure you that ...`, `I have taken the time to ...`, `I understand the importance of [following Wikipedia's rules], particularly (the ones) regarding ...`, `I recognize that ...`, `Going/Moving forward, I will ...`, `my aim/goal/intention is to ...`.
- People want to appear well-intentioned. Chatbots do it in a specific way: invoking rules, guidelines and standards as a broad, formal, abstract whole, like legalese, often with AI vocabulary.
- Examples: `I understand the importance of adhering to Wikipedia's policies, particularly regarding self-promotion`; `I genuinely value the collaborative nature of Wikipedia and strive to contribute positively`; `I am more than willing to make any necessary adjustments to ensure compliance`; `committed to creating informative and balanced content that adheres to Wikipedia's guidelines`; `create a valuable resource that aligns with Wikipedia's high standards`; `I will ensure my contributions are constructive, neutral, and policy-compliant`; `will work within the community's expectations going forward`; `My intention has always been to contribute constructively`.
- Search patterns: `I will ensure (that) my edits/contributions are ... and`; `Going/Moving forward, I will ensure (that) my`; `I have taken the time to ______ the rules`.
##### Denial of promotional intent
Words to watch: `My intention is not to promote` [a person or organization] `but to` [provide information about them].
- When accused of conflict of interest or promotion, users deny it, usually as a "not X but Y" negative parallelism, and say they are only sharing information.
- Examples: `my intention is not to promote any specific company or product`; `The article aims to shed light on`; `not created for promotional purposes but to contribute to the knowledge base`; `The goal here is not promotion but to share information`; `written in a neutral, encyclopedic tone and does not contain advertising language, calls to action, or subjective praise`; `It is not intended to promote the company, but to document it in an informational and neutral manner`; `My objective is not to promote any business, brand, service, or commercial activity`.
- Web relevance: disclaimers of the form "we are not X, we are Y" in copy.
##### Dubious assurance of human curation
- When caught, users admit AI use and then assure they checked it or that the words reflect their own thoughts. Examples: `I may use tools to help organize ... every response I post reflects my own intent`; `language refinement via AI tools ... Content selection, structure, and sourcing were entirely human-led`; `Language was refined using AI tools ... the content, structure, and sources were all human-curated`; `I rely heavily on ChatGPT, but I am most certainly human!`.
##### Assertions of notability
- Where articles stress impact, authors' comments overstate how well the topic meets notability rules. Example: `Lovern is a notable figure within the comic book industry, particularly for his pioneering role ...`.

#### Uncertainty about content issues (and requests for input)
Words to watch: `If [there are/you have] any concerns regarding ...`, `If there are specific [areas/examples/passages/sections] that ...`, `I [would appreciate]/[welcome] (any) [additional/further] feedback/guidance/input/suggestions`.
- A user whose draft is declined or tagged acts as if they cannot tell what is wrong, and asks which passages are at fault so they can fix them. Canned willingness to improve is common in AI comments; the difference from a conscientious human shows once the criticism is actually given.
- Examples: `Your assistance in this matter would be invaluable`; `welcome any additional input that can help us improve the article`; `If you have specific suggestions ... we would greatly appreciate your input`; `If you have any particular sections you'd like us to address, please do not hesitate to let us know`; `our willingness to address feedback`; `Which specific passages raised concerns about possible LLM-generated content? Are there particular statements ...?`; `Understanding the specific basis for the concern would help me revise the draft`.
##### Canned requests for third-party review
Words to watch: `[Can/Could/Would] an [experienced/independent/uninvolved] [editor/administrator] (please) [advise/review] ...`.
- Stock request for an "experienced", "uninvolved" or "neutral" editor or administrator to give a second opinion. Requests for source assessments carry an implication that the reviewer did not do the job.
- Examples: `Would an independent editor please review the article`; `I respectfully request that an administrator: Review the article and its sources. Provide guidance ...`; `could an uninvolved editor please check out the current draft and advise`.
##### Confusion over the reason for a declined draft
Words to watch: `Is the (main/primary) concern/issue (related to) ...?`, `I am trying to understand whether the (main/primary) concern/issue/problem [relates to / is (related to)] ...`.
- Chatbots cannot read the decline notice, so generated questions express uncertainty about a reason that is clearly stated, sometimes contradicting themselves (acknowledging the reason, then asking whether it is really the reason).
- Examples: `I'm not looking to dispute the reviewer's decision. I genuinely want to understand what policy I'm misunderstanding`; `is the main concern that claims about the subject need stronger sourcing ... rather than the subject's notability itself?`; `Any guidance on which sources should be kept, downgraded, or removed would be appreciated`.

#### Dismissal of AI usage accusations as unfounded
Words to watch: `accusing/accusations/accusatory`, `impression(s)`, `interpretation`, `speculation/speculative/speculating`, `subjective`, `unsubstantiated/unsupported/unwarranted`.
- When challenged, users call it speculation about tone or style rather than hard evidence, and tell critics to point out what to improve and focus on that.
##### Requests to focus on content quality instead
Words to watch: `dismissing/dismissals/dismissive`, `instead of/rather than ...`, `let's [focus / keep this (discussion) focused] on ...`.
- Frequently negative parallelisms: `Let's keep this focused on X rather than Y`; `Instead of X, this discussion has veered off into Y`; `the correct course of action is to X, not Y`.
- Answers to a pattern accusation: call it speculative, baseless, uncivil, hostile, dismissive, unprofessional, counterproductive. Persistence is treated as aggression; users may announce they are leaving because the discussion is "emotionally charged", then return soon after. AI rarely confirms it is AI unless the user lets it; if a reply is vague, sidesteps or treats the question as a personal attack, that may be a sign.
- Example phrasings: `Instead of making accusations, let's focus on fixing this article`; `I consider your accusations to be frustrating and inconvenient`; `This discussion has shifted from productive discourse to accusatory remarks`.

#### Misrepresentation of Wikipedia's policies and guidelines
- Chatbots give wrong or outdated information about rules, sometimes crediting a rule to a shortcut whose target does not contain it, or crediting a related page's content to the cited guideline. Example: users citing a guideline for "permitted uses" such as using AI to "spot structural problems", "generate ideas" and ask "how to improve a paragraph", which appear in an overview page and not at the cited shortcut.
##### Non-existent shortcuts
- Pasted text with shortcuts that redirect nowhere. Sometimes lists of real-looking shortcuts with one-line glosses (`WP:AGF assume good faith`, `WP:BITE do not bite newcomers`, `WP:INVOLVED avoid actions where involved`).
##### Minimization of Wikipedia's prohibition on AI use
- When confronted, users downplay or deny the rule against generating content with AI and argue that compliance with other content rules matters more than how the text was made.

#### Exhaustively procedural language
Words to watch: `adhere to`, `align with`, `ensure (that)`, `in compliance/line with`.
- Users who propose or offer edits go into detail on how they follow (or would follow) the rules or how the article complies. They also "spell out" core policies or misquote pages or cite missing shortcuts.
- Examples: `We remain committed to creating content that aligns with Wikipedia's mission`; `combines a rigorous analysis with real-world examples`; `engaging with the Wikipedia community`; `maintaining the integrity of Wikipedia`; `are the result of human effort and collaboration`; `made significant revisions in response to the feedback`; `ensured that all sources are credible and properly cited`; `worked hard to ensure the article is neutral and well-sourced, with a clear distinction between fact and opinion`; `participating in the community review process and making our article the best it can be`.
##### Overjustification of editing suggestions
Words to watch: `These edits/changes would make the article ...`, `[Performing this action] would enhance the article's ...`.
- Users defend proposed edits by saying they improve policy adherence, tone, formatting, structure or reader convenience, echoing the chatbot habit of telling users that an action will "improve" the article.

### Language and grammar
#### Excessive use of shortcuts
- Citing several policies via shortcuts (wikilawyering, bludgeoning) to argue that style cannot justify removal. Some shortcuts do not say what is claimed or do not exist, or are just "WP".
#### "Spelling out" of Wikipedia's core content policies
- Naming a few core policies together, often with shortcuts: `verifiability (WP:V), neutral point of view (WP:NPOV), and reliable sources (WP:RS)`; `Wikipedia's core content policies including WP:V (Verifiability), WP:RS (Reliable Sources), and WP:BLP`; `core policies, including verifiability, neutrality, and no original research`.
#### Frequent use of certain words or phrases
Words to watch: `concrete` (as an adjective), `I'm happy to ...`, `I [appreciate/look forward to/welcome] (any) contributions/feedback to ensure the article meets Wikipedia's guidelines`, `I respectfully ...`, `I understand [e.g. the importance of following Wikipedia's policies]`, `particularly regarding ...`, `Thank you for your time and consideration`.
- A catch-all for phrases not tied to one other sign.
##### Concrete (as an adjective)
- Users confronted over LLM use demand "concrete examples" of what to fix or "concrete evidence" beyond "stylistic indications", then dismiss accusations as subjective speculation. Humans also use the word for abstract versus concrete.
- Examples: `Without concrete financial data`; `Where is the concrete evidence linking me to these subjects?`; `In the absence of concrete evidence, I propose removing the AI-generated tag`.
##### "I am happy to..."
- Willingness to make whatever changes are needed once told what to improve. Examples: `I am happy to discuss this process with the appropriate editors or administrators`; `I'm happy to go through the article section by section and show where each claim comes from`; `I'm happy to manually polish the phrasing going forward so it reads as naturally as possible`; `I am happy to follow that process`.
##### "I respectfully..."
- Users "respectfully" request actions or challenge cleanup. Examples: `I respectfully contest the proposed speedy deletion`; `I also respectfully disagree`; `I respectfully request clarification of the evidence`.
#### Failed attempts to cite project pages
- Pasted links lose the colon and the first word after it, so citations read as just "WP" (`per WP, WP and WP`).

### Style
- Formal salutations, valedictions and subject lines are common after 2023 in AI text, but letters and emails have always used them. New editors or school tasks may write formally. A formal style with no other signs is not reliable. "I just write in a formal style" does not excuse a pile of signs.
#### Subject lines
- Comments sometimes begin with email-style `Subject:` text. Examples: `Subject: Request for Permission to Edit Wikipedia Article - "Dog"`; `Subject: Edit Request for Wikipedia Entry`; `Subject: Request for Review and Clarification Regarding Draft Article`; `Subject: Concerns about Inaccurate Information`. Recurring starters: `Subject: Appeal Against/for/Regarding`, `Concern(s) About/Regarding`, `Request to`, `Urgent`, `Request for Account/Assistance/Clarification/Feedback/Guidance/Permission/Reconsideration/Removal/Review/Unblock`.
- Web relevance: email-style subject and greeting leftovers in contact copy or email templates.
#### Division of text into titled sections
- Messages split into titled sections, as Markdown or plain text, sometimes resembling inline-header lists. Example section titles: `Risk of Violating Wikipedia's Policy on Biographies of Living Persons`, `The Dangers of Blindly Cutting Content`, `The Role of Due Diligence in Editing`.
##### Misuse of section subheadings
- Level 2 or 3 subheadings in comments, sometimes Markdown `##`. Examples: `Key Issues Requiring Community Review`, `Prior Cleanup Efforts`, `Closing`, `What I am requesting`, numbered headings like `2.2.1 The Open Dou: The Primordial Form`. In replies, indentation can stop headings from rendering.
#### Transclusion of article maintenance banners
- Writing template names in braces (`{{Disputed}}`) makes the banner render inside the comment. Wikipedia-specific.

### Miscellaneous
#### Chat log disclosures
Words to watch: `You said:`, `ChatGPT said:`.
- Pasted chat logs including prompts, which may reveal the user's own unassisted style. Examples: `You said: ... ChatGPT said: Certainly! Here is a detailed message you can use ...`; `Saved memory full`; `Could you please clarify what you're referring to by "What does it mean?" ...`.
#### Canned unblock requests
- Typical text: `Dear Wikipedia Administrators, I respectfully request that the block on my account be lifted. I acknowledge that my recent behavior has been in violation ... My intention was to edit constructively, and I sincerely apologize for any disruption I may have caused. I have taken the time to carefully read and review all of Wikipedia's policies and guidelines ... Moving forward, I am committed to ensuring ... Thank you for your time and consideration.` Combines assurance, willingness and feedback requests, all canned. See also [`identifying-llm-unblock-requests.md`](identifying-llm-unblock-requests.md).
##### Generic language
Words to watch: `I understand Wikipedia's policies/guidelines regarding ...`, `If unblocked, I will ...`.
- The chatbot knows little about the user's case, so the text could fit any blocked user: generic promises of constructive, policy-based editing. Examples: `I have carefully reviewed Wikipedia's policies and guidelines regarding promotion and advertising`; `I genuinely value the principles and integrity of Wikipedia`; `I kindly request your reconsideration`; `I humbly request another chance`; `I appreciate your time and attention in reviewing my case`.
##### Apologies for potential past damage
Words to watch: `I apologize for any ...`, `may have ...`, `could be interpreted/perceived as ...`.
- Apology for "any" disruption they "may have" caused, not the specific disruption that led to the block, implying doubt that they did wrong. Examples: `any violations I may have unintentionally committed`; `some of my previous contributions may have inadvertently crossed the line`; `some of my edits may have appeared promotional`; `my username could be interpreted as representing an organization`.
- Web relevance: hedged apologies or claims ("may have", "could be perceived as") in notices, error pages and policy copy.
#### Canned ANI reports
- Reports at an administrators' noticeboard written by LLMs: each issue as a list entry or titled subsection with title-case headings, bold or attempted Markdown bold. Example: numbered bold labels such as `1. **Account Age:**`, `2. **Prior SPI Context:**`. The format has its own essay (not retrieved; see [`README.md`](README.md)).
#### Sealioning
- Feigned ignorance with relentless demands for evidence, then accusing targets of incivility. Several AI users do this when confronted: long comments, lists or sections, requests for examples of problematic text or stronger evidence than "stylistic indicators", and charges of "unsubstantiated speculation" and incivility.
#### Wikilawyering
- Selective policy citing against the spirit of rules. Chatbots tend to produce text that affirms what the user wants others to believe. Typical moves: affirm that the content adheres to policies; stress that the AI guideline allows basic copyediting or translation; deny that AI writing is prohibited; claim accusations need hard evidence beyond style (sometimes citing the "AI detection ability" caveat); ask critics to point to specific passages; cite many shortcuts.
- Reported case: users claimed the LLM guideline lets AI "spot structural problems", "generate ideas" and "improve a paragraph". Those words appear in a different overview page, which says the ban does not cover indirect use adjacent to editing, with the reminder that chatbot information is unreliable.
